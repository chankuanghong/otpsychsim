# Intentional Relationship Model (IRM) Knowledge Cheatsheet

## 1. Core Framework Overview

The Intentional Relationship Model (IRM) explains how the client-therapist relationship can facilitate or hinder a client's occupational engagement. It acts as a complementary layer to existing occupational therapy conceptual practice models.

### Four Central Elements of IRM

- **The Client:** The focal point of the therapeutic interaction.
- **The Interpersonal Events:** Inevitable, naturally occurring therapy events that carry emotional charge.
- **The Therapist:** Responsible for bringing interpersonal skills, therapeutic modes, and reasoning into the interaction.
- **The Occupation:** The desired task or activity selected for the therapy session.

## 2. The Underlying Principles

1. **Critical self-awareness** is key to the intentional use of self.
2. **Interpersonal self-discipline** is fundamental to effective use of self.
3. It is necessary to **keep head before heart**.
4. **Mindful empathy** is required to know your client.
5. Therapists are responsible for expanding their **interpersonal knowledge base**.
6. Provided that they are purely and flexibly applied, a wide range of therapeutic modes can work and be used interchangeably.
7. The client defines a **successful relationship**.
8. **Activity focusing** must be balanced with **interpersonal focusing**.
9. Application of the model must be informed by **core values and ethics**.
10. Application of the model requires **cultural competence**.

## 3. Understanding Client Interpersonal Characteristics

Therapists categorize interpersonal characteristics into two dimensions so they can dynamically modulate their interactions.

| Attribute Type | Definition | Clinical Implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Situational** | Emotions, behaviors, and reactions that are context-specific and inconsistent with standard behavior, usually linked to acute medical or systemic stressors. | Normative acute reactions to stress; does not reflect character or baseline traits. |
| **Enduring** | Baseline emotions, behaviors, and traits that are consistent across time and circumstances. | Reflects personality, preferred communication styles, and baseline capacity for trust. |

### 12 Core Categories of Client Variation

- Communication style
- Capacity for trust
- Need for control
- Capacity to assert needs
- Response to change and challenge
- Affect
- Predisposition to giving feedback
- Capacity to receive feedback
- Response to human diversity
- Orientation toward relating
- Preference for touch
- Capacity for reciprocity

## 4. The Six Interpersonal Therapeutic Modes

Therapists develop a therapeutic style based on their preferred modes, but should strive to be multimodal so they can match changing clinical circumstances.

| Mode | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Advocating** | Ensuring client rights are enforced, legal protections are recognized, and environmental resources are secured. |
| **Collaborating** | Treating clients as active, equal participants in joint decision-making; encouraging autonomy and personal choice. |
| **Empathizing** | Striving to understand the client's inner cognitive and emotional perspectives deeply, without passing professional judgment. |
| **Encouraging** | Acting as a source of positive reinforcement; evoking hope, courage, and playfulness to stimulate behavior. |
| **Instructing** | Assuming a clear educational style; structuring therapy explicitly, setting behavioral limits, and providing directed active feedback. |
| **Problem-Solving** | Relying on pragmatic, strategic, and logical reasoning; using tools such as Socratic or strategic questioning to safely explore options. |

### Critical Guideline: Pure Mode Application

Modes should be used purely rather than blended simultaneously. Blending modes, such as trying to empathize while also giving corrective instruction, can send mixed messages, cause confusion, and fracture therapeutic trust.

## 5. Inevitable Interpersonal Events

Inevitable interpersonal events are naturally occurring, emotionally charged occurrences that can either threaten or strengthen the therapeutic relationship.

- Expression of strong emotion
- Intimate self-disclosures
- Power dilemmas
- Nonverbal cues
- Crisis points
- Resistance and reluctance
- Boundary testing
- Empathic breaks
- Emotionally charged tasks and situations
- Limitations of therapy
- Contextual inconsistencies

## 6. The Six Steps of the Interpersonal Reasoning Process

This systematic process supports a self-disciplined and intentional response to clinical dilemmas or baseline interpersonal challenges.

```text
[ Step 1: Anticipate ]
          |
          v
[ Step 2: Identify and Cope ]
          |
          v
[ Step 3: Determine if Mode Shift is Required ]
          |
          v
[ Step 4: Choose Response Mode or Sequence ]
          |
          v
[ Step 5: Draw on Relevant Skills ]
          |
          v
[ Step 6: Gather Feedback ]
```

### Step 1: Anticipate

- **Action:** Cultivate ongoing mental vigilance. Assume that interpersonal adjustments, client behavioral barriers, or emotional threats are likely to emerge in any interaction.
- **Focus:** Familiarize yourself early with the client's situational and enduring vulnerabilities to avoid an unexpected knee-jerk personal reaction.

### Step 2: Identify and Cope

- **Action:** Name the specific interpersonal event that has occurred. Label it silently or process it out loud with the client.
- **Self-management:** Take a dynamic breath to collect emotional objectivity. Intentionally manage internal impulses and avoid personalized defensiveness.

### Step 3: Determine if a Mode Shift is Required

- **Action:** Evaluate whether your current interpersonal style or primary therapeutic mode remains effective for the immediate demands of the moment.
- **Decision matrix:** Ask, "Is continuing in my current mode serving the client's expression, or is it feeding a non-therapeutic gridlock, such as an instructional power struggle?"

### Step 4: Choose a Response Mode or Mode Sequence

- **Action:** Formulate an intentional verbal or nonverbal statement using a clean, unblended response mode.
- **Sequencing:** For layered dilemmas, map an explicit sequence. For example: first use the Empathizing mode to validate an injury, then transition cleanly into Problem-Solving or Instructing once the client feels safe.

### Step 5: Draw Upon Relevant Interpersonal Skills

- **Action:** Map your chosen mode directly to specialized skills from your interpersonal knowledge base.
- **Core domains:** Deploy targeted skills such as strategic questioning, boundary setting, resolving empathic breaks, or understanding maladaptive systemic dynamics.

### Step 6: Gather Feedback

- **Action:** Explicitly or observationally check in with the client immediately after your response.
- **Validation:** Confirm whether the client feels validated, safe, and ready to re-engage with the primary target occupation. Work toward mutual interpersonal repair if an unexpected break persists.

## 7. Operational Blueprint: Resolving Empathic Breaks

An empathic break occurs when a therapist's behavior or unexpected oversight is perceived by a client as hurtful or insensitive.

Use the **Empathizing Mode** and apply these six actions:

1. **Recognize:** Stay vigilant to subtle shifts in client affect, nonverbal signs, or direct behaviors that signal injury.
2. **Avoid Minimizing:** Do not deflect, offer premature optimization, or tell the client "it's not that bad."
3. **Avoid Inflaming:** Prevent defensiveness or arguments from escalating the rupture.
4. **Raise It Openly:** Proactively name the observation with the client and bring the event into a safe space for dialogue.
5. **Repeat Your Understanding:** Mirror back the source of the injury explicitly, validating the client's right to feel hurt.
6. **Soothe and Reassure:** Accept responsibility for the oversight and offer a calm, professional apology if indicated. Ensure the client feels heard before attempting task remediation.
